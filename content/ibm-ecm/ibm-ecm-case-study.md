# Emptortoise

*Emptoris Contract Manager — IBM · Interaction Designer · 6 months · 2015*

---

Sarah, a contract manager at a regional healthcare network, has ten contracts in negotiation and needs exactly one of them: the master agreement she was working on yesterday. The search screen is waiting. It has fifty-two required fields, and she knows maybe three of them. She fills in what she can, hits search, and gets nothing back. She tries again with different guesses. Somewhere in her inbox is an encrypted Word copy of the same contract — she keeps one for every deal now, because at least her email search works. She opens Outlook.

The product is Emptoris Contract Manager, IBM software that thousands of companies use to run the full life of a legal contract — creating, negotiating, approving, executing, and amending agreements between parties. For contract managers like Sarah, it's where the record of every obligation lives; for their employers, it's how deals close on time and stay legally sound. By 2015 the product was fifteen years old and hadn't had a serious redesign in its life. Its users had a nickname for it: the Emptortoise — for how slowly it ran and how poorly it functioned. The Emptoris team, caught up in the momentum of IBM's new design thinking program, asked for help with one thing: make contract search easy. They expected it to take a month. We negotiated three, with an option to continue.

> **Problem:** Finding a contract in Emptoris Contract Manager meant filling out a form with fifty-two required inputs — and even then the search succeeded only 53 percent of the time, after about five minutes of effort. Contract managers, whose entire job is moving many agreements forward at once, couldn't reliably find their own work, so they fell back on Word documents and encrypted email threads outside the system of record. Every workaround eroded the product's reason to exist, and it showed in the business: renewals were slipping in a suite sold to Fortune 500 legal and procurement teams.
>
> **Goals:** A contract manager can find any active master agreement — payment terms and all — within four clicks. She can reach 80 percent of her work within one click of the homepage. Overall, cut the time to find a contract and return to work by 55 percent.

The evidence came fast. We interviewed more than twenty-five contract managers, and the language barely varied: "I can't find anything." "I hate how many clicks it takes to do anything." "There are several different interfaces, and I'm not sure I trust it." The core team was three designers and two product managers working with forty-five developers — inside a larger suite organization of six products whose architecture was, by the development team's own admission, fragile and overdue for restructuring. The whole team met twice a week to keep the goals honest.

The idea that reorganized everything arrived over Christmas break, away from the office. The old search asked its fifty-two questions before showing anything. Every search engine Sarah used outside of work — Amazon, Google — did the opposite: answer first, refine after. The redesign stopped being about better forms at that moment. Type a few characters, get auto-complete suggestions, recent searches, and saved searches immediately; run it, and a full results page with filters and tags appears for refining. The product would answer first and ask questions later.

The homepage followed the same logic: a dashboard of the contracts Sarah is actually working on, each with its status and stage visible, so she — and her managers — could see where every negotiation stood and jump back in. One fight got cut short by a constraint worth keeping: users wanted every data column at once, and no pattern I could find supported that. I capped the table at five columns and made them user-configurable — defaults narrowed through testing. The development team, staring at their fragile backend, initially balked at building any of it; we worked with product management to phase the search experience across releases instead of demanding it all in version one. When the visual design came, we pulled back from our early wilder concepts toward flat, familiar data tables — closer to the e-commerce patterns users already trusted. Over two months we ran usability tests with thirty-two users. Then Emptoris extended the engagement three more months for the harder problem: six products in the suite, each on a different front-end framework. I started coding components from our style guide myself, built them into a CSS framework with our visual designer, and worked with the development leads on a migration plan to carry the experience across all six products.

Sarah's morning now starts on a homepage that already knows her contracts. In testing, 81 percent of users found a contract in under a minute and under five clicks — against a five-minute, coin-flip baseline — and 94 percent reached 80 percent of their work within one click of the homepage. Clause review lagged at 62 percent, honest evidence of work remaining. Users rated the experience four out of five. Within six months of release, the renewal rate rose from 66 to 72 percent across fifty-one Fortune 500 customers, twenty-five new customers signed, and the product earned more than $85 million in revenue. The fix was never teaching users to search better — it was teaching a fifteen-year-old product to answer first and ask questions later.
