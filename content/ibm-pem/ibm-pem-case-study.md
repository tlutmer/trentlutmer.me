# A Sigh of Relief After D-Day

*IBM Partner Engagement Manager · Design Lead · 12 months · 2013–2014, released Q1 2015*

---

Cindy, a line-of-business representative at a corporate bank, is watching a date get closer. Weeks ago her team launched an onboarding campaign — a new trading partner, thirty-eight touchpoints, dozens of tasks spread across email threads, phone calls, faxes, and systems that don't talk to each other. Somewhere out there, people are watching training videos, updating certificates, filling out forms. Or they aren't. She has no way to know. What she knows is the deadline, and the ritual that comes with it — as one of her peers put it: "Many times, we're not entirely sure how the campaign is currently going. We just heave a sigh of relief after D-day."

The product built to end that ritual is IBM Partner Engagement Manager. It organizes, tracks, and manages a company's campaigns — onboarding programs, compliance pushes, SSL certificate updates — across its partners, customers, and suppliers, in one place instead of everyone's inbox. In 2013, when the design work began, the numbers explained the sighing: onboarding a single corporate banking client took 25 days on average, and clients were running roughly 1,500 new setups a month. One user counted 38 touchpoints and 60 extra services on a single onboarding, with millions of interactions flowing underneath. Every manual step was a chance for an error, and in banking, errors compound into missed revenue and regulatory exposure.

> **Problem:** Teams running B2B campaigns couldn't see them. Work rolled out through emails, calls, faxes, and disconnected systems, so line-of-business reps didn't reliably know when a campaign started, who owned it, or whether it would hit its deadline — they found out how it went after it was over. Trading partners on the receiving end couldn't tell which tasks were theirs or what was left. The cost was structural: 25-day onboardings, people deployed to mask the time lag, and errors multiplying at every manual entry point — in an industry where a missed step can mean fines.
>
> **Goals:** Give monitoring tools that speed campaign completion by 200 percent. Let a trading partner find their tasks and start working in under 60 seconds, down from about three minutes. And let companies maintain their own B2B systems, without leaning on IBM Support.

We interviewed fourteen users across two weeks, and the pain came back in their own words: "Onboarding takes too long, and we often use people to mask the time lag." "Information comes in from everywhere and lives in multiple systems." I led a team of four designers, working with two product managers and a forty-five-person development organization — a team that had never worked with designers before, in 2013, when IBM's design thinking program was in its infancy. We wrote a mantra to keep everyone pointed the same way: remove barriers by driving collaborative relationships. From the research I built personas for both sides of the relationship — the rep who tests every campaign before it ships, down to checking the quiz answers, and the trading partner who just needs to know he's watched four of the six videos in chapter nine.

From two "how might we" questions — automate the manual process; surface problems and troubleshoot blocked tasks — I pitched two experiences: a dashboard and an activity designer. At first, it did not go well. Product management and engineering raised concerns and wanted more features. The turn came in those discussions, when the team accepted the premise underneath the pitch: two experiences that solved the majority of problems, finished and demonstrated well, would win more executive support than a roadmap of everything. Depth first, breadth after.

That principle shaped everything downstream. We met daily and concentrated on a single workflow at a time. The dashboard made campaigns, tasks, and trading partners visible at a glance and reloaded each user's last task so they could pick up where they left off. The activity designer let teams build any campaign from templates and components — a deliberate rejection of the tedious drag-and-drop builders of the era — and had to scale to campaigns with many steps and many people. When development pushed to start visual design early, I made the case that the pixels could wait until the story held together; when visual design's moment came, we flattened the interface toward the emerging IBM Design Language and washed back our colors so client banks could brand the tool as their own — trust, in that industry, wears the customer's logo. Testing with eleven users kept us honest, and iteration surfaced what the first version missed: campaign creation needed split tasks and rich text before it felt complete.

Cindy's D-day now starts with a dashboard instead of a deadline: every campaign, every task, every partner's progress, visible while there's still time to act. In testing, a five-step campaign took 9 minutes 43 seconds on average with an 81 percent self-completion rate; users left, returned, and found their last task in 27 seconds — down from about three minutes, less than half the 60-second goal; and client onboarding fell from 25 days to five. Eight sponsored users carried the beta, we shared the work with more than 200 people at the Smarter Commerce conference, and the product released in Q1 2015 — work recognized with an IBM Eminence & Excellence Award. The sigh of relief didn't disappear because campaigns stopped being hard. It disappeared because relief is what you settle for when you can't see — and now they could.
