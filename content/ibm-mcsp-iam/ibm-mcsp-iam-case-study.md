# Eight Consoles Deep

*Multi-Cloud SaaS Platform: Identity & Access Management — IBM · Product Designer · January 2023 – June 2024 (beta)*

---

Marcus, an IT administrator at a midsize manufacturer, has a new engineer starting Monday. All she needs is access to the tools her team already uses. Marcus opens the first of eight admin consoles and starts working down the list: an invite here, a role request there, a terminal window for the permissions that can only be drawn by command line. By lunch he has filed approval requests in four different systems and granted nothing yet. He types carefully — last quarter, one mistyped role took three days to unwind. Somewhere between console five and six, he stops being sure which permissions he's already requested.

Marcus's employer, like 90 percent of large firms, runs products across multiple clouds — IBM, AWS, Azure, Google. The Multi-cloud SaaS Platform (MCSP) is IBM's answer to what that does to administration: one platform where companies manage their accounts, subscriptions, billing, identity, access, and single sign-on, with common tools underneath to speed up product development. The urgency was set in May 2022, when IBM and AWS signed a Strategic Collaboration Agreement — IBM customers could now buy IBM SaaS products and host them on AWS. More clouds, more marketplaces, more subscriptions: the administrative sprawl was about to multiply. I was the product designer responsible for researching and defining MCSP's identity and access management.

> **Problem:** System and IT administrators used more than eight separate systems to buy and manage their IBM SaaS subscriptions, and drawing clear identity-and-access boundaries meant manual work in a CLI — redundant entries, avoidable errors, and approval chains for everything. Getting one new hire productive meant a chain of requests and approvals across systems with overlapping features but separate consoles, separate approvers, and separate ideas of what a role is — a finite game of request-and-approval in which no single step looked unreasonable and the sum was. For admins, that meant slow onboarding and risky mistakes; for IBM, redundant operational costs and missed sales. With the AWS agreement multiplying where subscriptions could live, the sprawl was about to get worse.
>
> **Goals:** Let IT and system admins identify, authenticate, and authorize any resource or entity — securely, on a single platform. Simply: who is this person? Are they allowed here? What are they allowed to do? Alongside it, the business strategy: boost presence in multi-cloud marketplaces, provision natively on cloud infrastructure, and cut development time to market by 60 percent.

The team assembled around the problem was itself a coordination exercise: designers, product managers, developers, architects, and content designers spread across six time zones. My research surfaced the two problems above quickly — the 8+ systems, the CLI-drawn boundaries — but agreeing on what to do about them was slower. The systems we were consolidating had overlapping features and their own opinions about how identity should work. For weeks, the team circled the same conversations about goals and outcomes without landing.

The fix for a team that couldn't stop talking past each other turned out to be a meeting where nobody talked. I proposed a silent working session: everyone writing, in parallel, what we knew, what we didn't, how admins did this today, and how we expected it to work tomorrow. Without the loudest voice setting the frame, the overlaps became visible on the board. I consolidated the insights, shared them back, and the plan that had eluded us for weeks fell out in one session: Phase 1 would be identity management — users and user groups — plus role-based access controls, built as microservices so each IBM product could adopt exactly the pieces it needed.

The building was less tidy. Our first major iteration mirrored parts of another IBM product, hoping to move faster on proven patterns — but when we walked it through with the team, very few of those parts meant anything in MCSP's context. Expecting that, I had already started a Plan B: identity flows mapped straight from the architecture documents — inviting, adding, viewing, editing, and removing users. Wireframes went in front of colleagues, and their feedback named the real monster: scale and visibility. "I can't tell who is receiving permission and what they will get." Access management — the who-gets-what-on-which-scope problem, spanning accounts, subscriptions, instances, users, groups, and service IDs — drove most of our twelve-plus major iterations. It also produced the project's hardest disagreement: my design manager proposed four sequential dropdowns for granting access; I proposed Carbon's Add/Select component, which showed the whole grant visually before you committed to it. We debated for months. The decision went to the dropdowns. It wasn't my favorite outcome — but I documented my alternative and kept it in the file for the day the question reopens, and we moved forward together into the final Figma components with visual design.

Marcus's Monday now starts in one place: invite the engineer, assign her group, grant the group its access — one platform, one flow, no terminal window. The beta shipped in June 2024, eighteen months after the January 2023 start. Six IBM SaaS products adopted it, with fourteen more in the queue; the shared framework cuts product development time by 60 percent, go-to-market deliverables move five times faster, and redundant teams and projects fell eightfold. SSO, federated identity, and just-in-time provisioning are next. The platform's real product was never the screens — it was one answer to the three questions every system had been asking separately: who are you, are you allowed here, and what can you do?
