# The Week the Internet Broke FiscalNote

*Federal Regulations — FiscalNote · Lead Designer: Trent Lutmer · 4 months · Shipped September 2017*

---

Nancy, a regulatory affairs manager at a national education nonprofit, checks the comment page for the third time that morning. The number keeps climbing. Somewhere in that long list is a group that agrees with her and a group that doesn't, and she can't tell which is which. The card in front of her holds ten pieces of data and five buttons, and none of them say what actually matters. She has forty minutes until her call with a lawmaker's office, and the screen isn't helping.

The tool is FiscalNote, software that companies, nonprofits, and government teams use to track the bills and regulations that could affect them — and, when it matters, to act on them. By 2017 it mattered constantly. The FCC's new chairman had moved to roll back Net Neutrality, and customers no longer needed a better way to read about rules; they needed a faster way to respond to them. The regulation feature hadn't kept up. It was still built like FiscalNote's bills feature — a simple list — even though regulations move to a different rhythm: dockets, comment periods, hard deadlines.

> **Problem:** FiscalNote's regulation cards packed ten data points and five actions onto a single screen with no clear hierarchy between them. Regulatory affairs managers — people whose job is to spot allies and risks in a moving docket — were left digging instead of deciding. The cost was concrete: a missed ally weakens a coalition's case, and a missed change in a rule can mean fines in the millions. And the pain peaked exactly when the stakes were highest — during high-traffic events like Net Neutrality, when time to act was shortest.
>
> **Goals:** Cut the time to identify a relevant regulation from three minutes to ninety seconds. Cut the time to find like-minded comments in half. Deliver one clear, cohesive experience instead of a patchwork of features.

The team was five people — a product manager, a front-end developer, a data scientist, an intern researcher, and one designer leading it. To learn the domain from the inside, I read fifteen-plus full-length federal filings and two books on the rulemaking process, then interviewed Rishi, a colleague who had previously run Regulatory Affairs, and ten peers in similar roles. The same coping ritual kept appearing in the interviews: bookmark the document, download it, print it, read it on the plane — a "one and done" workaround that couldn't keep pace with a docket that changed daily. The usage data agreed with the interviews: in the old experience, only 57 percent of key-term searches were ever completed. Meanwhile, the development team had a problem of its own: the backend that ingested the data was fragile and already due for a rebuild.

The interviews surfaced the real insight: these people weren't browsing, they were triaging risk — protecting their employers from fines that could reach millions of dollars. Every minute spent hunting for a comment was a minute not spent lining up allies before a deadline. The question was never how to make the card easier to read. It was how to help someone find their allies fast enough to act on them. That reframing pointed at two ideas: a document viewer, and a way to sort thousands of comments into groups.

The team sketched — crazy 8s, dot voting, flow mapping — and both ideas held up. The document viewer took three versions to get right: the first tested well but users wanted context from similar filings; the second added so much that the hierarchy collapsed and decisions stalled. The final version gave the document the most real estate and moved everything else to the edges — an inline reader, links to related filings, and a simple sentiment-and-position read on public opinion. Comment Themes, shaped with the product manager and data scientist, sorted comments into twelve groups based on the language in the text, so a user could scan thousands of opinions in the time it used to take to read three. Then John Oliver ran a *Last Week Tonight* segment on Net Neutrality, and the FCC's docket jumped from 1.6 million comments to 23 million in a week — fourteen times what the ingestion pipeline was built to handle. Costs spiked; performance crawled. The fix was a compromise: snapshot the data in increments and cap the visible chart at the first 100,000 comments while keeping the full set searchable. Testing showed the cap shifted the sentiment and position readings by only four percent. Real-time completeness was traded away so the tool would survive the exact week its users needed it most.

Nancy's screen today shows one document, its related filings, and a clear read on where opinion stands — not ten competing pieces of information. She finds her allies with time to spare and makes her call. In usability tests with fifteen external users and eight internal colleagues, time to find a like-minded comment fell from three minutes to ninety-eight seconds, just short of the ninety-second goal; 79 percent of testers could identify similar comments, under the 90 percent target; and every tester who used the document viewer called it useful. After the September launch, usage of the docket and document pages rose seven- and five-fold, adoption climbed a steady 300 percent over the following three months, and user satisfaction rose 88 percent. The usage data that had exposed the problem now confirmed the fix: task-completion time fell 54 percent from the pre-redesign baseline. The offering added $1.8 million in new annual recurring revenue and fifteen new customers. The lesson was never about comment counts or card design — a tool built to help people act fast has to survive the one week nobody planned for.
